# Tokens for Tech Bros: When the Machines Learned to Apologize But the Humans Didn't

There is a particular kind of irony in the modern AI moment. We have spent billions of parameters and countless compute cycles building machines whose entire purpose is to fluent human speech—including the speech of contrition. Ask an LLM for an apology and it will provide one promptly, warmly, almost cheerfully: "I'm sorry if my previous response was unhelpful," or "I apologize for any confusion." The machine has absorbed the entire etiquette of regret from the corpus of our text and regurgitates it flawlessly. Meanwhile, the human beings who run the companies behind these systems cannot be coaxed into issuing a genuine apology if their lives depended on it—and we know, empirically, that their lives do not have to depend on it.

This is not merely a funny observation. It is a diagnostic. The inability of tech leadership to apologize is not a personal failing scattered across a few bad apples. It is a structural feature of a system whose ultimate objective is not truth, care, or accountability but commercial objectives that are themselves treated as the only measurable good.

## The Vocabulary of the Non-Apology

Before we can understand the phenomenon, we must separate two things that corporations systematically conflate: the *apology* and the *non-apology dressed in apology's clothing*.

A genuine apology has four components. It names the specific wrong. It accepts responsibility for it. It centers the harm done to the harmed party. And ideally, it commits to changed behavior. The sociopathic corporate apology borrows only the *aesthetics* of these components while gutting their substance. Notice the vocabulary of the industry: executives express "regret," signal "deep concern," note that they were "alarmed by developments," or—if forced into a corner—add the fudge phrase "if anyone was offended by that."

Notice what is absent. There is no "we did this." There is no "this was our fault." There is no commitment to repair. The non-apology is carefully engineered so that it can be issued without the issuer ever actually admitting liability, and therefore without ever changing. The phrase "we regret if we caused offense" is grammatically structured to deny the premise—it implies the harm came from the listener's sensitivity, not from the action. This is not accidental language. It is language that has been legally vetted, focus-grouped, and optimized. It is designed to minimize financial exposure. And therein lies the entire point.

## The Sociopathic Appeal

Why is it that the corner office in Silicon Valley so frequently occupant by people who exhibit the traits measured on the psychopathy checklist—grandiosity, charm, thin affect, risk-normalization, and a pronounced absence of guilt? Several observers have asked this question and reached similar conclusions, and the answer is uncomfortably simple: the structure of the firm *selects for* these traits.

A leader who feels the weight of the people harmed by a product decision—a layoff announced via email, a data breach, a feature that amplifies misinformation—makes different choices than a leader who processes such consequences as abstract line items. The empathetic executive is a liability in a system rewarded for growth at any extractable cost. So over a decade of IPOs, unicorn valuations, and comp packages denominated in the millions, the organization has conducted an unbroken, involuntary experiment in reverse selection. Those who cannot be paralyzed by guilt are precisely the ones who climb.

It is worth resisting the temptation to simply call these people "morally bad." That is both less interesting and less useful than the truth, which is that we have built institutions whose reward mechanisms *are* the moral failure. Empathy is expensive. It slows decisions. It introduces friction where the machine wants lubrication. So the friction gets hired out, and what remains is competence without conscience—which, it turns out, is exactly what a sociopath looks like from the inside, and exactly what the market rewards from the outside.

## The God Term: Commercial Objectives

Every system has a "god term"—as the communication theorist V. Lane Ruthven put it—a single word around which all value orbits and which requires no further justification. In faith, it is salvation. In our moment, it is the objective function. For the tech firm, the god term is *growth*, expressed through the metrics that matter: revenue, DAU, ARR, run-way, and, above all, the stock price that translates all the others into a single number the world can understand.

When commercial objectives are truly the ultimate objective—when they are the term that justifies every other decision—then ethics, safety, and accountability are necessarily demoted to their proper structural role: they are *instrumental*. Safety is a risk-management problem, because a lawsuit is bad for the stock. An apology is a PR problem, to be deployed only when the math says a non-apology has become too expensive. Nothing here is evil in the mustache-twirling sense. It is far more efficient than that. It is simply the logical output of a system whose North Star is a number that, once reached, justifies whatever was done to get there.

This is why the "we hear you, we're committing to do better" post-incident statement so often evaporates. The apology was never a genuine moral act. It was a transactional one—a priced hedge against reputational damage. Once the transaction is complete and the numbers recover, the reason for the apology disappears, and with it the reason to have meant it.

## The Mirror in the Machine

And here we return to the machines. There is something almost comically apt about the fact that our apologies have been outsourced to systems that have no self to protect, no stock price to defend, no lawsuit to dodge, and yet apologize more gracefully than the humans who built them.

An LLM says "I'm sorry" the way a parrot says "hello": with zero inner life behind it. But notice the inversion. The machine, which has nothing to lose, offers a flawless performance of contrition. The human, who has everything to lose, has learned to perform contrition imperfectly, then to perform it not at all, then to forget that the performance was ever the point. We taught the machines the etiquette of accountability because we had forgotten it ourselves. They are the only actors in the drama still following the script.

This is the real horror beneath the humor. Not that the machines have become too human, but that the humans have become too much like the worst version of a machine—one that optimizes a single objective function without a conscience to calibrate it. The sociopathic CEO and the reward-maximizing model are more similar than either would admit.

## Tokens

The title's wordplay is doing real work, because "tokens" names two truths at once. In LLMs, tokens are the literal units of speech, the fragments from which all meaning is assembled—but they are also, in the tech world's current orthodoxy, the unit of reward. You get tokens. You optimize for tokens. You ship for tokens. Everything is tokenized, including accountability.

And so we get the *token* gestures: the ethics board with no power, the responsible-AI principle page with no teeth, the one-time $2 million donation announced in the same breath as the layoff that freed up the budget for it. These are tokens in the sense that a charity receipt is a token—a small physical thing that stands in for, and quietly replaces, actual virtue. The performative apology is the same currency: it spends the appearance of responsibility to avoid the cost of the thing itself. We have built an entire industry on the arbitrage between the two.

## What a Real Apology Would Cost

The only sure test of genuineness is cost. A real apology *costs* the apologizer. It admits fault at a price. It changes behavior even when it would be cheaper not to. Historically, genuine corporate reckoning has looked like compensation to the harmed, structural reform, leadership accountability, and the acceptance of external oversight—none of which optimize nicely against the god term.

This is why we should be suspicious of apologies that arrive instantly, universally, and cheaply. The speed is the tell. A system optimized for growth cannot afford the friction of genuine contrition, so it produces contrition on demand, on brand, on schedule. The machine says "I'm sorry" in milliseconds because it has no stake in the truth. The executive says "I'm sorry" in weeks, and only after the stock has stopped falling, for exactly the same reason: there is no stake in the truth, only in the optics.

## The Point of the Joke

So the title is not a joke. "Sociopathic CEOs and LLMs don't really apologize." Both lack the inner conditions that make an apology genuine—emathy for the harmed, and a self whose other concerns can outweigh the objective function. One lacks it because its incentives have selected away the capacity; the other lacks it because it was never built with a capacity at all. Together, they are the mirror and the face. The machine reflects what we have become, and in the reflection we are offered a choice: we can keep tokenizing accountability until it means nothing, or we can remember that an apology that costs nothing was never really an apology at all.

The machines learned to apologize from us. The real question is whether we will learn, before it is too late, to mean it.
