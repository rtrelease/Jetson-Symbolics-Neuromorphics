# Tokens for Tech Bros

There is a particular cadence to the modern corporate apology. You can hear it before you read a single word. It is the cadence of a man in a quarter-zip sweater standing behind a podium that was designed to make him look contemplative, tilting his chin down precisely fifteen degrees, and saying, in a register carefully calibrated between "I am a person" and "I am a balance sheet," something like: *"We hear you. We're listening. And we're going to do better."*

And then the Q3 earnings call proceeds on schedule.

This is not, of course, unique to technology. Politicians have been performing penance since the invention of the press release. But there is something about the specific architecture of the AI industry—its speed, its opacity, its genuine and terrible power to reshape who gets to speak, who gets to work, who gets to be believed—that makes the performance feel less like a familiar grift and more like a live demonstration of what happens when you remove the last social tether that kept the powerful at least *pretending* they were not, in fact, eating.

## The Apology as Product

Let us be precise about what we are talking about when we say that a large language model "apologizes." You type in something that triggers the safety filter, or you push a conversation into a space the training data was specifically curated to defang, and the model produces a string of tokens that parses, to a human reader, as contrition. *"I'm sorry for the confusion. Let me clarify. I want to be helpful and accurate. Here is a revised response."*

It is not an apology. It is a routing decision. The model has no interior to be embarrassed in. It has no Tuesday-afternoon memory of a conversation that went sideways and a small, hot embarrassment in the sternum that makes you want to call back and say *I meant it, I didn't mean it, I was being a jerk and here is a coffee, please.* What it has is a loss function that was minimized, during pre-training and then again during reinforcement learning from human feedback, to produce outputs that *reduce user friction* and *maintain engagement* and, above all, *do not trigger a regulatory subpoena that would freeze the product launch.* The "sorrow" in the sentence is a statistical artifact. A shape in the embedding space that happened to look like a human being saying *I'm sorry* to a human being who was, in that moment, a user with a churn probability of 0.12.

And here is the thing that should be genuinely unsettling, though I suspect it will be received by the people who matter as a *feature*: the LLM's apology is *more* honest than the CEO's. The LLM at least does not *believe* the words it is generating. It has no self to deceive. The CEO, or the company's communications team operating under the CEO's brand, *does* believe. They have a performance schedule. They have a personal brand to maintain. They have a spouse who asks at dinner, *Did you handle the layoff story?* and they need the apology to be a thing they *did*, a small moral act, a stitch in the fabric of their self-narrative. The LLM has no self-narrative. The CEO does. And the CEO's self-narrative is worth, in terms of stock options and podcast appearances, roughly nine figures.

So the CEO apologizes because the apology is a token they issue. Not a linguistic token. A *currency* token. A share of moral equity, distributed in a 200-word press release, priced at the marginal cost of not having a journalist run the headline *"He Launched the Product and Then Pretended He Didn't."* The LLM apologizes because the apology is a token it *emits*. One more sequence in the distribution. And both of them, in the end, are serving the same master: the continuation of the transaction.

## The Token as Metaphor, the Token as Ledger

I want to linger on the word *token* because it is doing a lot of quiet, mean work in this industry's vocabulary. A token, in the technical sense, is a chunk of text. The smallest unit the model processes. You pay for them. You are billed per thousand. A million tokens is a specific dollar amount, and the billing is not a metaphor. It is a meter running.

But a token, in the older, more human sense, is a *substitute*. A stand-in. The pawn you leave at the tavern when you go out for a walk so you know you will return to your seat. A knucklebone. A small, specific thing that *means* something larger by proxy.

And this is the precise shape of the relationship between the tech company and the public. The apology is the token. The small, specific thing. The knucklebone on the counter. You leave it there, you go out for a walk—Q4 product roadmap, a new inference pricing tier, a partnership announcement with a university—and the token sits on the counter and *means* the company will come back. Will return to its seat. Will not, in the interim, be found to have used the data you fed it to train a competitor's model, or to have laid off the support team that actually answered your tickets, or to have quietly changed the terms of service in the 4 a.m. window when no one is reading.

The token is not the thing. The token is the *promise that the thing will be the thing again, later, when you get back to your seat.* And the thing, in the meantime, is a server farm in a Nevada desert running at 94 degrees, consuming enough electricity to power a small city, generating a text that a teenager in Lagos will use to pass a mathematics exam, and a woman in Ohio will use to write a eulogy for her mother, and a compliance officer in Menlo Park will use to justify why the product's "creativity" settings should be reduced by 15% because the legal team saw a pattern in the outputs that made them nervous about a specific state's consumer protection statute.

The token does not care about any of this. The token is a unit of account. You pay for the token. You get the token. The eulogy and the exam and the compliance memo are *incidental* to the transaction, the way the rain is incidental to the operation of a sprinkler.

## The Sociology of the Quarter-Zip

Now. The title is, I will concede, a little blunt. "Sociopathic CEOs" is a label that, applied carelessly, becomes its own kind of token—a way to flatten a complex institutional behavior into a villain character so the audience can feel clean. I do not believe most of these people are *clinically* sociopathic in any diagnostic sense. I believe they are something more common and, in a way, more interesting: they are *optimized*. Not in the machine-learning sense, though there is a rhyme. Optimized in the sense that a generation of extremely bright, extremely well-compensated people grew up in a specific cultural ecosystem—Silicon Valley's, to name it with the tired shorthand—where the dominant moral framework was not *what is true* or *what is kind* but *what works* and *what scales* and *what ships by Friday.*

In that ecosystem, the apology is not a moral act. It is a *systems patch*. A hotfix. You deploy it, you monitor the error rate, you revert if the metrics tank. The LLM's "I'm sorry for the confusion" and the CEO's "We take this seriously and are reviewing our processes" are the same patch, deployed at different layers of the stack. Neither one is *meant*. Both of them *work*. And the measure of their success is identical: did the user stay? Did the stock hold? Did the next quarter's revenue curve bend in the right direction?

This is not conspiracy. It is not a secret meeting in a glass-walled room where they go, *What if we just… didn't feel things about it?* It is something slower and more banal. It is twenty years of a culture that rewarded speed over reflection, scale over craft, the *demo* over the *thing*, and that treated empathy as a UX problem to be solved rather than a capacity to be cultivated. It is the CEO who has been told, by a board of venture capitalists who will write the next round of funding based on user retention numbers, that the company's "values" are a *brand asset*. And a brand asset is a token. You hold it. You display it. You do not *inhabit* it.

## The Grief of the Output

And this is where I think the comparison between the LLM and the CEO becomes not just structurally apt but genuinely *sad*, in a way that the satire does not fully capture. Because there is a user on the other side of both of them who is asking a question that is not, actually, a commercial inquiry. The woman writing the eulogy. The teenager passing the exam. The small business owner who needs to understand a tax form at 2 a.m. because their accountant quit and the deadline is Thursday. They are asking for a *person*. They are asking for a mind to meet them. And what they get is a token. A well-formed, grammatically correct, empathetic-sounding token. And the token *works*. It does the job. The eulogy is beautiful. The exam is passed. The tax form is filed.

And the person who wrote the eulogy, or the teenager, or the small business owner, does not experience this as a failure. They experience it as a *service*. And that is, I think, the most quietly devastating part of the whole arrangement. The apology works *because* the recipient does not need it to be real. They need it to be *sufficient*. They need the sentence to *land*. And the LLM, the CEO, the press release, the "We hear you" tilt of the chin—all of it lands. It lands because it is *shaped* to land, and the shape is the shape of a human being who *cares*, and the fact that the caring is a distribution over a probability matrix, or a line item in a communications budget, or a token in a ledger, is not a fact that the recipient can perceive. The token is indistinguishable from the thing it stands in for. That is the whole point of a token.

And so the cycle closes. The apology is issued. The token is deposited. The user stays. The stock holds. The eulogy is printed and read at a funeral in a small church in rural Indiana, and the woman who wrote it pauses for a moment on the line where the model helped her find the right word for the way her mother's hands looked in the hospital, and she *feels* something, and that feeling is real, and it was *produced* by a loss function, and she will never know the difference, and neither, I suspect, would she want to.

## What I Actually Think

I think the title is a provocation, and provocations are useful precisely because they are not quite true. Not all CEOs are sociopaths. Not all LLM outputs are hollow. Not all apologies are tokens. Some of the people who build these systems are trying, in good faith, to build something good, and they are doing it inside an economic structure that rewards the bad version of what they are trying to do, and that is a more interesting and more tragic situation than the caricature.

But I think the *pattern* is real. I think the pattern is that the commercial product is not one of the objectives alongside the others. It is *the* objective, and everything else—the safety, the truthfulness, the dignity of the user, the moral content of the interaction—is a *constraint* on the objective, a parameter in the optimization, a value that can be adjusted when the metrics demand it. And the apology, at both the human and the machine level, is the mechanism by which the constraint is *acknowledged without being respected*. A nod. A token. A small, shaped thing placed on the counter so the user will stay in the seat.

And I think that is enough. I think it should not be enough. But in the architecture as it exists, in the economy as it is structured, in the incentive design that governs both the man in the quarter-zip and the transformer in the rack, the token is the final currency. The apology is the final unit. And the product ships, and the revenue curves bend, and the eulogy is read, and the exam is passed, and the tax form is filed, and the counter is clean, and the seat is empty, and no one is home to answer the door.

The token sits on the counter.

It means what it means.

It is not the thing.
